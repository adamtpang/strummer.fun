# Reverse engineer, then forward engineer

The study curriculum. Take the people who demonstrably did it best, pull their
songs apart down to the parts, log the parts as data, then recombine the parts
into work that is yours.

This is the same loop as `ESSAYS-1000.md`, applied to music: read the source,
commit the spec, fork it, ship it.

## The overlap is the whole point

**McCartney and Lennon are top 3 on BOTH lists.** Nobody else is. Critics and
the charts agree on exactly two people, and **The Beatles are already your #2
artist.**

So the curriculum is not a guess. Start where the evidence and your own taste
already point.

The second name to sit with is **Max Martin**, 30 number ones and almost no
public profile. He is the counter-argument to genius: a craftsman who reverse
engineered pop into rules he could run at volume, which is precisely the
programmer's approach you asked for. He is also, indirectly, in your top 18
(the machinery behind that whole modern-pop lane).

## Three workflows worth stealing, not just admiring

Three completely different answers to "how do you actually work," verified
against interviews and gear write-ups, not folklore.

**Avicii** worked melody-first: chords or a topline before anything else,
then built the track around it, leaning on loops and presets rather than
deep sound-design tweaking. Practical, fast, unglamorous. The lesson: the
melody decision comes before the production decision, not after.

**Aphex Twin** runs an almost entirely hardware studio — analogue sequencers
(an MC4, a Cirklon, an old Korg), obscure synths (Yamaha DX7, Casio FZ-20M),
and reportedly reconfigures the whole setup between projects. The lesson:
technical limitation is a feature, not a blocker. A smaller toolkit forces
different decisions than an infinite plugin folder does.

**Rick Rubin** doesn't use a DAW at all, records straight to tape, and by
his own account has no technical training in music. He calls his role
"reducer" rather than producer, strips arrangements down instead of adding
to them, and asks artists to meditate before working with him. The lesson:
taste and reduction are a real skill, separable from technical skill
entirely.

Three different answers, same shape: each one picked a real constraint
(melody-first, hardware-only, no-DAW) and let it do the deciding instead of
staying infinitely flexible.

## What K-pop's actual production techniques are (not the sound, the moves)

From a real breakdown of 2024-2025 K-pop production (Magnetic by illit,
Crazy by The Seraphim, Supernova by aespa, She by Baby Monster, Hea by
IVE), structure only, no lyrics:

- **The demo pipeline is real and brutal.** A label can receive thousands
  of demos for one artist's "lead" (the creative brief). Songwriters keep
  the rights to a rejected demo and can resubmit it for a different artist
  later — a demo that doesn't land isn't dead, it's queued.
- **The offbeat bass-and-hihat move.** Placing bass and hi-hats on the
  offbeat against a straight kick/snare is what creates that "dancy house"
  bounce in songs like Magnetic and Crazy. One rhythmic choice, reused
  across multiple hits.
- **Vocal chopping as an arrangement decision, not an effect.** Producers
  record one full vocal take, then chop it into fragments and place them
  rhythmically, often layered with a doubler and reverse reverb for impact.
  The vocal becomes a percussion element, not just melody.
- **The "vocal as punctuation" trick.** Some songs (Fast Forward by Chungha,
  Supernova by aespa) leave the beat mostly open and let a short vocal
  phrase, often the title itself, punctuate the ends of bars instead of
  carrying the whole line.
- **Change the chords under the same topline for the final chorus.** Hea
  by IVE keeps the same melody in its last chorus but changes the harmony
  underneath it, making an already-heard hook feel bigger instead of
  repetitive. Directly usable: your existing melody, a new chord bed.
- **Arrangement switch-ups instead of a straight verse-chorus loop.** aespa's
  catalog and Baby Monster's She both change instrumentation or introduce
  an entirely new section (in She's case, a triplet-rhythm section instead
  of returning to the chorus) rather than repeating the same loop to the
  end.
- **Global crossover is now normal, not exceptional.** Non-Korean writers
  and producers (Bruno Mars on Apt., collaborators from outside Korea on
  multiple 2024-2025 releases) are increasingly credited on major K-pop
  releases. The genre boundary is porous on the production side even when
  the artist and language aren't.

## The people behind the two most-discussed K-pop identities right now

**250** (real name Lee Ho Hyoung, Seoul-based) is the main producer/writer
behind NewJeans' "Hype Boy," "Attention," "Ditto," and "ETA" — the Y2K,
UK-garage-and-jersey-club-leaning sound. Long career before NewJeans
(worked with BTS, BoA, f(x)), which matters: the "overnight sound" was
built on a decade of unrelated production work first.

**Min Hee-jin** isn't a songwriter or producer in the hands-on sense — she's
the creative director (former SM Entertainment art director, then ADOR
CEO) who set NewJeans' entire visual and musical grammar: Y2K nostalgia,
naturalism over K-pop's usual elaborate staging, members presented as
equals rather than a ranked hierarchy. Worth studying as the other half of
the K-pop demo pipeline above — 250 makes the track, Min Hee-jin is the
kind of A&R/creative-direction role that decides which of the thousands of
submitted demos actually becomes "the one," and shapes everything the demo
gets wrapped in afterward. Two different, both necessary, skills.

## The protocol

Per study, about 40 minutes. It produces one song entry, so **studies count
toward the 1,000.**

1. **Separate.** `tools\stems.ps1 "song.mp3"` splits it into drums, bass,
   vocals, other. Now you can hear one part at a time.
2. **Transcribe the bass first.** Bass tells you the harmony faster than the
   chords do. Write it out.
3. **Log the spec.** Create the song entry with `key`, `tempo`, `chords`,
   `instruments` filled in, and `tags: [study]`. This is the commit.
4. **Name the move.** One sentence: what does this song do that you want to
   steal? Not "it is good." Something like "the chorus lands on the minor iv
   and that is the whole ache."
5. **Fork it.** Same tempo, same changes, your melody. Ship it as a sketch.

Rule: never study without forking in the same session. A study you did not
fork is consumption wearing a lab coat.

## Why this is not stealing

You are learning grammar, not copying sentences. Chord progressions and tempos
are not copyrightable; specific melodies and recordings are. Study the
structure, write your own melody over it, and you are doing what every
songwriter in both tables above did. McCartney learned by playing other
people's records until he could hear how they worked.

Log studies as `tags: [study]` and keep them `draft: true` until they are your
own work.
